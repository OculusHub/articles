# Distance Vision Test Apps for iPhone and iPad: A 2026 Re-Review

This app review revisits a peer-reviewed study published in JAMA Ophthalmology in January 2021: [Visual Acuity Testing for Telehealth Using Mobile Applications](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7809609/) (Steren, Young and Chow). 

## The verdict

Of the 13 apps in the JAMA candidate list, **4 have disappeared from the App Store** and **3 have not been updated in years**. Only a handful are still maintained and genuinely built to test distance vision.

- **Best all-rounder: Eye Charts (formerly Visual Acuity Charts).** It had the lowest measured optotype error in the JAMA study (0.2%, SD 0.0%). It was updated November 2026 (v4.0.1), and it has the widest choice of charts, notations and test distances.
- **Best for young children and clinical use: Kay iSight Test Professional.** Second most accurate in JAMA (3.5%, SD 0.7%), with picture optotypes, a medical-device registration and result export. It is subscription-based beyond a free three-line test.
- **Usable as a rough complement: Eye Chart Pro (iPad only) and Eye Chart HD (iPhone).** Neither is positioned by its developer as a primary measuring tool.

One important caveat up front: the JAMA study checked only whether each app drew letters at the correct physical size. 

## What the JAMA study measured

The authors searched the US App Store between 17 April and 5 May 2020 for free, English-language, non-entertainment apps that stated their test distance. They then compared each app's expected optotype size at various acuity lines against the size actually measured on four Apple devices. Errors were calculated per device and averaged across devices.

**Remarks:**
* Eye Charts (US$4.99), Eye Chart Pro (US$99) and Kay iSight Test Professional (US$12.99/Month) are not free.

Key results from the paper:

- On iPhones, 10 apps qualified, with mean errors from 0.2% to 109.9%.
- On iPads, 9 apps qualified, with mean errors from 0.2% to 398.1%.
- Six apps worked on both device types, with mean errors from 0.2% to 249.5%.
- The three most accurate cross-device apps were Visual Acuity Charts (0.2%, SD 0.0%), Kay iSight Test Professional (3.5%, SD 0.7%) and Smart Optometry (15.9%, SD 4.3%).
- The authors concluded that none of the apps was suitable for telemedicine.

## Accuracy: how the apps measured up

The full paper is on [PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7809609/) and the [JAMA Network abstract](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamaophthalmology/article-abstract/2774801).

What the verified numbers support:

1. **Visual Acuity Charts is in a class of its own.** An error of 0.2% with an SD of 0.0% means the chart was essentially the correct size on every device tested, with no device-to-device variation. Of the 6 cross-device apps, the worst had a mean error of 249.5%, which is more than double the intended optotype size.
2. **Kay iSight Test Professional is also very good.** A 3.5% mean error with a small SD of 0.7% is within the range most users would consider negligible for screening. Its consistency across devices matters because the same app can be run on different iPads and iPhones.
3. **Smart Optometry is a clear third, but a noticeable step down.** An average error of 15.9% (SD 4.3%) means letters were drawn noticeably off-size. For a measurement tool, that is a meaningful source of error.

**Remarks** 
* The study tested 2020 app versions. Eye Charts has since been renamed and is now v4.0.1, and Kay is at v7.1. Their current accuracy is likely at least as good.
* The apps that have not been updated since 2016 to 2020 are the ones whose 2020 results most likely still apply.

Observations:

- **Eye Charts has the strongest practicality story.** It is the only app whose description explicitly mentions self-illumination and adjustable brightness, which matters because a dim screen at 3 to 6 m makes small optotypes hard to see regardless of size accuracy. It also offers the most notations and a self-test option.
- **Kay is the most clinically oriented.** Crowded optotypes and picture tests help with children who cannot name letters, and results can be saved and exported. On iPhone, the maximum distance is 3 m, which limits full 6 m (20 ft) testing to iPad.
- **Randomisation helps repeat testing.** Eye Charts, Eye Chart Pro, Eye Chart HD and EyeTesterFree all vary the optotypes, which reduces memorisation. For a child tested weekly or monthly, this matters.

## Which app should you use?

**Home users and parents of children aged 6 and over:** Start with **Eye Charts**. A child of 6 may not know all letters, so the Tumbling E, HOTV and Shapes options are useful, and the progress graphs let you see change over time. Keep the test distance fixed and measure it with a tape. Use the self-illuminated screen in a room without glare. 

**Healthcare professionals:** Use **Eye Charts** for general screening and trend records, and 

**Kay iSight Test Professional** where picture optotypes, crowding, export, or iPad remote display are needed. Kay's registration as a medical device is a point in its favour, but it is self-described, so check the status for your jurisdiction.
